## Hambledon Hoard DETECTIVAL

This metal detecting rally was held near Henley on Thames and there was around 900 detectorists from the Uk and Europe.

What started out as a good weekend of meeting friends and generally having a good time turned at the beep of a metal detector into an outstanding weekend of metal detecting finds.

Initially one gold coin was found and then silver hammered coins and then more gold and more silver, totalling thus far 12 GOLD NOBLE coins and in the region of 600 silver hammered coins from around the midd 1200's.

Standing on that Buckinghamshire field in **April 2019** with the lens rolling as the **Hambleden Hoard** emerged from the turf remains one of the most electric experiences of my detecting career.

Set beside **Hambleden**, a picturesque village recorded as far back as the **1086 AD Domesday Book**, the rally started like any other crisp spring weekend. 

Nobody on that field expected to be standing over the biggest coin hoard unearthed in the UK in over a decade.

The Signals That Changed Everything: 16s and the Elusive 21 In metal detecting, most hoards don't announce themselves with a single dramatic sweep—they unravel signal by signal, heart-rate spike by heart-rate spike.

**557 Total Coins Recovered:** A massive medieval deposit representing a small fortune in 14th-century England.

12 Extremely Rare Edward III Gold Nobles: Minted between **1346 and 1351** during the height of the **Black Death**, these large, intricately struck gold coins are among the pinnacle finds of British detecting.

Filming the Hambleden Hoard Live: Standing Over 557 Medieval Gold & Silver Coins in Buckinghamshire.

 There are moments behind the camera when you realise you aren’t just filming a [weekend rally](https://www.paulcee.co.uk/detecting-rallies/), you are witnessing British numismatic history rewrite itself in real time. 

{{youtube id="49lbEIUvU3c" title="Hambledon Hoard Of Gold Coins"}}

On the day the hoard broke open, the detectorists working the patch started hitting repeatable **16 target IDs ,** that classic, unmistakable signature of hammered silver—followed by the elusive **21 signal** that every detectorist dreams of hearing for gold. When the turf first came back, two medieval coins sat waiting in the soil.

 One glance deeper into the pit made it immediately clear this wasn't a scattered purse loss—the ground was packed with centuries-old wealth. By the end of the first day alone, **nine gold nobles** and **276 silver coins** had been recovered from the ground. By the time the excavation concluded, an astonishing **557 medieval gold and silver coins** had been brought back into the daylight for the first time since the 14th century.

Inside the Hambleden Hoard: Gold from the Era of the Black Death What makes the Hambleden Hoard extraordinary isn't simply the sheer volume of silver and gold—it is the rarity and historical weight locked inside the mintage.

{{youtube id="https://youtube.com/live/vauNSRHsiMk" title="Hambledon Hoard Gold and silver Hammered Coins"}}

According to British Museum curator **Barrie Cook**, only **12 examples** of these 1346 and 1351 Edward III gold nobles were known to exist prior to the Hambleden discovery—meaning this single Buckinghamshire field effectively doubled the known surviving specimens overnight. **_Early Hammered Silver_**: _Among the very first coins lifted from the pit were **12 ornately decorated silver pennies**_ struck during the reigns of **Edward I and Edward II (1272–1327)**. 

A Rare Regional Mintage Mix: Alongside the English royal issues, the silver coins featured a remarkable spread of mints from **Lincoln, Birmingham, Ireland, and Scotland**, painting a vivid picture of coin circulation across medieval Britain and Ireland.

 Behind the Lens: Capturing Raw History Without a Second Take Filming a live hoard excavation is pure adrenaline mixed with intense focus. When a coin that hasn't seen sunlight since the 1350s is gently eased out of dark Buckinghamshire earth, there are no second takes. 

Through the viewfinder, every detail tells a story: the mud-caked edges of hammered silver catching the April light, the sudden, unmistakable flash of 14th-century high-purity gold cutting through the soil, and the stunned disbelief on the faces of everyone gathered around the pit. Someone buried this fortune during one of the most turbulent, plague-ravaged chapters in English history—intending to return when the danger passed—and never made it back. Being there to document the discovery as it unfolded meant preserving not just the artefacts themselves, but the raw, unfiltered human emotion of the moment they were found.

##  Frequently Asked Questions About the Hambleden Hoard 

When and where was the Hambleden Hoard found? 

The Hambleden Hoard was discovered in **April 2019** during an organised [Metal Detecting Rally](https://www.paulcee.co.uk/detecting-rallies/) in a field near the historic village of **Hambleden in Buckinghamshire**, England.

###  How many coins were in the Hambleden Hoard?

 A total of **557 medieval coins** were excavated from the hoard, comprising **545 silver coins** and **12 rare gold nobles**.

####  Why are the Edward III gold nobles from Hambleden so significant? 

The 12 gold nobles date from **1346 to 1351**, the exact period when the **Black Death** devastated England. Before the April 2019 Hambleden find, British Museum curator Barrie Cook noted that only 12 examples of these specific 1346 and 1351 Edward III gold nobles were known to exist.

 What types of silver coins were included in the hoard? 

The silver coins included hammered issues from the reigns of **Edward I and Edward II (1272–1327)**
