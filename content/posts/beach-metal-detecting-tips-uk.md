## Beach Metal Detecting Tips

Knowing where to swing your coil makes the difference between going home with a pocket full of ring pulls or finding old coins and lost jewellery. Most detectorists walk straight onto the sand and start swinging blindly, but learning **how to read a beach** before you even switch your detector on will dramatically improve your results.

In this guide—and in my accompanying video below—I break down the exact tidal clues, sand layers, and seasonal hotspots I look for on UK beaches.

## How to Read the Beach: Natural Clues & Sand Movement

Beaches are constantly shifting. Wind, spring tides, and storms move tonnes of sand overnight, either burying targets out of reach or stripping the beach bare to expose older, heavier finds.

* **Watch the Tide Go Out for Low Spots:** If you detect near an estuary or anywhere with a large tidal range, watch the water recede. As the sand dries, high spots lighten while \*\*low spots\*\* stay darker and hold water longer. Heavy targets naturally wash down into these depressions.

* **Look for Black Worm Casts:** Lugworm casts are normally brown, so if you spot **black worm casts** on the surface, the worms are pushing up shallow **black sand** from just underneath. Heavy minerals and older targets settle in black sand layers—if you see black casts, slow down and grid the area thoroughly.

- **Work Around Rocks, Boulders, and Old Rubble:** Boulders and exposed old rubble act as natural traps for heavy items washing along the shoreline, and they also indicate historical activity. Fit a smaller coil—like the [[Minelab Manticore M8 or M9 coil]](/blog/m8-vs-m9-coil) to get cleanly into the nooks and crannies between rocks.

- **Find the Exposed Clay Layer:** When heavy erosion strips away the top sand, it often reveals the underlying **clay layer**. Coins, weights, and older relics cannot sink through solid clay, meaning targets sit right on top of it.

- **Read Ripple Sand (and Know What to Avoid): Ripple sand** forms where a strong current or tide has shaved off the top few inches of sand. While it holds plenty of lightweight foil and can shards you have to work through, it regularly produces great finds. Conversely, avoid **soft slurry sand** (freshly dumped sand that has washed off the main beach) and thick piles of **seaweed**, which usually mean material has been pushed ashore rather than stripped away.

## Use Fixed Landmarks to Track Sand Levels

Every time you visit your local beach, take a quick smartphone photo of fixed structures to track whether sand is building up or washing away:

* **Groynes and Sea Walls:** Check the height of the sand against wooden groynes and promenade walls. If you can see a couple of feet more wall or piling exposed than on your last visit, the beach has stripped back and it is time to detect.

## Dry Sand & Summer Beach Hotspots

During warm weather, busy UK beaches replenish themselves daily with fresh drops. Always wait until after teatime when the crowds head home so you can detect undisturbed.

- **The Towel Line & Water's Edge:** Where parents stand in the shallows with toddlers, cold water shrinks fingers and rings slip straight off. Working the wet edge and the main towel line after the crowds leave is one of the highest-percentage spots on any beach.

- **Up Against Promenade Walls:** People sit leaning against sea walls all afternoon, and coins, keys, and lighters regularly slip out of shorts and trouser pockets. Because sea walls contain steel rebar that will overload your detector, **turn your sensitivity right down**. You are only looking for fresh surface drops, so low sensitivity lets you sweep right up to the concrete without picking up the internal steel.

- **Wading in the Shallows:** If you are using a waterproof machine like a [[Minelab Manticore]](/blog/manticore-settings), Minelab Equinox, or CTX 3030, wading knee-to-chest deep where swimmers gather puts you over targets dry-sand detectorists cannot reach. Even with a splash-proof detector like the [Minelab Vanquish 60 series](/minelab-vanquish-60-series/), you can detect safely in the shallows.

## Frequently Asked Questions: UK Beach Metal Detecting

## 

### What is the best time to go metal detecting on a UK beach?

The best time to detect a UK beach is at low tide, ideally two hours before to two hours after low water, especially following big spring tides or winter storms that strip away top sand. During summer, detect popular beaches after teatime once crowds leave to find fresh dry-sand drops undisturbed.

### What clues show that a beach is good for metal detecting?

Look for signs of sand erosion rather than build-up. Key clues include exposed clay layers, black lugworm casts (indicating shallow black sand), darker wet low spots holding water, ripple sand, boulders or old rubble, and visible tide marks showing sand has dropped against groynes and sea walls.

### Should you detect wet sand or dry sand on the beach?

Both produce well depending on the season and conditions. \*\*Wet sand\*\* near the low-tide mark, gullies, and clay ledges holds heavier older coins and lost rings, while \*\*dry sand\*\* around the towel line and sea walls is best in summer for fresh surface drops.

### Do I need permission to metal detect on UK beaches?

On most UK beaches, you do not need private landowner permission, as the foreshore (between high and low water marks) is predominantly Crown Estate land where metal detecting is generally permitted. Always check local council bylaws first, avoid Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSIs), and never detect on protected archaeological sites.

### How do I stop my metal detector falsing near concrete sea walls?

Turn your detector's sensitivity right down when sweeping close to concrete sea walls and promenades. Sea walls contain internal steel rebar that overloads high-sensitivity settings, whereas lower sensitivity ignores the deep rebar while still picking up fresh coins and jewellery dropped on the surface.

### Storm Hunting: Timing and Safety

Spring tides paired with strong onshore winds can strip feet of sand off a beach in a single tide cycle, exposing targets that have been out of reach for years. 

> **Safety First:** Never venture onto the shoreline during the height of a storm. Rogue waves and surging tides are dangerous and put both you and rescue crews at risk. Wait until the storm passes and the tide drops, then head out to clean up.
